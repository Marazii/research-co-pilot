## Drafted Discussion (first 800 words shown)

The headline finding admits a compact reading: in our panel, knowledge workers who reported higher mentorship quality during their first 12 months were more likely to remain at their employers at the 12-month mark, by an odds ratio of 1.34 (95% CI [1.12, 1.61]). The association attenuates when controlling for manager rating but does not vanish (OR=1.21, 95% CI [1.02, 1.43]) — a pattern that suggests mentorship contributes to retention beyond what is captured by managerial assessment alone.

That this association exists is, to be clear, not surprising. Pre-pandemic, the mentorship-retention link was already well-documented (Eby et al., 2008). What our data add is the observation that the link survives the transition to remote work, at least in the form measured by self-report. The more interesting question is what kind of mentorship is doing the work, and our qualitative analysis points to an answer that the survey instrument alone could not surface.

Across eighteen interviews, participants consistently distinguished mentorship — the offering of advice, skill development, and feedback — from sponsorship, the expenditure of social capital on a junior colleague's behalf in advancement decisions. This distinction is not novel; Hewlett (2013) made it precisely. What is striking is how cleanly the distinction maps onto the remote-work experience: participants described mentorship as having reconstituted, asynchronously, in Slack channels and pull-request reviews, but described sponsorship as having gone quietly missing. As one participant put it, summarizing what may be the central finding of this study: *"I didn't have the sense that anyone was pushing for me specifically. My manager was nice but they weren't an advocate."*

If this characterization is correct, the survey-quantified retention effect we observe is likely tracking mentorship in the narrower, advice-oriented sense — the function that scales to async channels. Sponsorship, the function that is plausibly more consequential for advancement and longer-term retention, is **[LITERATURE NEEDED — claim: "sponsorship is harder to reconstitute remotely than mentorship"; suggested search terms: "remote work sponsorship", "advocacy", "informal advancement networks"; likely body of work: organizational behavior on patron-protege relationships; possibly Higgins & Kram 2001 on developmental networks]** likely harder to reconstitute under conditions of geographic dispersion and absent incidental contact. Our quantitative analysis is silent on this; the qualitative analysis is consistent with it. A follow-on study with a behavioral sponsorship measure — perhaps coding who is included in promotion-decision conversations, accessible via platform admin data — would test this directly.

This raises a methodological point worth dwelling on. The standard mentorship instruments (Eby et al., 2008, foremost) do not separately operationalize mentorship and sponsorship. They were developed for an era and a literature that treated the two as closely related variants of a single construct. Our findings suggest that, in the remote-work context at minimum, this conflation may underestimate the importance of sponsorship and overestimate the extent to which surveyed "mentorship" captures the full developmental relationship. **[LITERATURE NEEDED — claim: "current mentorship instruments do not separately operationalize sponsorship"; suggested search terms: "mentorship scale validation", "sponsorship measurement", "developmental network instrument"; likely body of work: industrial-organizational psychometrics on developmental relationships]** Building a sponsorship-specific subscale, calibrated and validated for remote and hybrid contexts, is a contribution we leave for subsequent work.

A second methodological observation concerns where mentorship now happens. Participants consistently identified async channels — Slack, GitHub pull-request reviews, internal documentation — as the substrate on which mentorship operates under remote conditions. This is a relocation, not a disappearance. It is also, importantly, a relocation that *inverts the geography* of mentorship access: in the physical-office era, the mentors available to a junior worker were those on the same floor, in the same building, in roughly the same time zone. In the async-channel era, the mentors available are those who choose to be active in the channels the junior worker has access to — which may span multiple geographies and seniorities. The trade is incidental contact (lost) for breadth of access (potentially gained). **[CITATION NEEDED — describe the claim: empirical evidence that async-channel-active senior employees span wider geographies than office-proximate senior employees would]** The net effect on mentorship quality is, our data suggest, slightly positive in the mentorship-as-advice sense and substantially negative in the sponsorship sense.

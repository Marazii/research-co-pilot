## Cover letter to the Editor

Dear Dr. Hypothetical Editor,

Thank you for the opportunity to revise our manuscript. We have considered the comments from yourself and from both reviewers carefully and have prepared a substantial revision.

The most consequential changes are:

1. **Causal-language revision throughout.** We have replaced causal phrasing with associational language ("is associated with" rather than "causally increases") in the abstract, results, and discussion. R1's concern (R1.1) is correct, and the revision is more honest about what the cross-sectional design can establish.
2. **Full subgroup-analysis transparency.** We have added Table 2 with N per cell, effect sizes with 95% CIs, and Benjamini-Hochberg multiple-comparison correction. We have also explicitly labeled the subgroup and moderator analyses as exploratory (the study was not pre-registered).
3. **Moderator interpretation revised.** The discussion of the political-ideology interaction now reports the 95% CI on the interaction term and acknowledges the power constraint.
4. **Policy implications scoped to sample.** The discussion's policy recommendations are now explicitly scoped to populations resembling the college-aged sample, with generalization claims moved to a clearly-labeled "speculative implications" subsection.
5. **Literature engagement expanded.** Following R2.1, we have added engagement with Kraut & Resnick (2012) and three additional prior works in the literature review.

**One respectful pushback:** R1.1 and R2.1 disagree on the causal-language issue — R1 wants associational language, R2 considers causal language acceptable with limitations noted. We have sided with R1's position because the cross-sectional design genuinely does not support causal claims, and conservative reporting is the field's emerging norm. We hope R2 will find the revised framing strengthens rather than weakens the contribution.

Sincerely,
[Authors]

## Point-by-point response

### Editor's notes

#### E.1 — Reduce manuscript length by ~15%

> *Please reduce manuscript length by ~15% to fit the journal's standard format.*

**Response:** We thank the editor for this guidance. We have reduced the manuscript from 6,800 to 5,750 words (~15.4% reduction) primarily by tightening the literature review (now organized by idea rather than by source) and consolidating the methods section.

**Revision:** Throughout — primary changes in §1 (Introduction) and §2 (Literature Review).

#### E.2 — Confirm pre-registration status

> *Confirm pre-registration status — if not pre-registered, explicitly label all subgroup and moderator analyses as exploratory.*

**Response:** The study was not pre-registered. We have added explicit "exploratory" labels to all subgroup and moderator analyses in §3 (Results), and added a sentence in §2 (Methods) noting the non-pre-registration status. Future work will be pre-registered.

**Revision:** §2 (Methods), final paragraph; §3 (Results), subgroup analysis subsection introduction.

### Reviewer 1

#### R1.1 — Causal-language overreach

> *The causal language throughout the manuscript is not supported by the cross-sectional design. [...] Please revise to use associational language consistently OR provide identification strategy for a causal claim.*

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for this important critique. We agree that the cross-sectional design does not support causal claims, and we have revised the manuscript accordingly. The abstract now reads "predicts" rather than "causally increases"; the results section uses "is associated with"; the discussion now explicitly notes the limitation: "While these findings establish a robust association, the cross-sectional design cannot rule out reverse causation (members who trust news may seek out communities) or third-variable explanations." We also added a paragraph to the Limitations section emphasizing the design constraint.

We have not added a new identification strategy because the data are cross-sectional and we don't have an instrument or natural experiment available; we agree the right move is to honest about the association.

**Revision:** Abstract (lines 1-12); §3 Results (paragraph 1); §4 Discussion (paragraphs 1, 4, 6); §5 Limitations (paragraph 1).

#### R1.2 — Subgroup-analysis transparency

> *The subgroup analysis is reported as significant "for all subgroups examined" without listing them, providing cell sizes, or addressing multiple-comparison correction.*

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for this fair concern. We have added Table 2 to §3 (Results) reporting, per subgroup: N, effect size (standardized β) with 95% CI, raw p-value, and Benjamini-Hochberg-corrected p-value. Subgroups: age tertile (18-22, 23-28, 29-34), gender, education (HS or some college / Bachelor's / Master's+), and political-ideology quintile. We also note in the text which analyses were pre-specified (none) and label all as exploratory.

After Benjamini-Hochberg correction, the headline association remains significant within 4 of 9 subgroup cells; effect-size magnitudes range from β = 0.18 to β = 0.42.

**Revision:** §3 Results — new Table 2; §3 Results paragraph 3 — explicit pre-registration note and exploratory labeling. Cross-reference: this revision also addresses E.2.

#### R1.3 — Moderator interpretation

> *The non-significant moderator finding (p = .12) is interpreted as showing the effect is "uniform across the political spectrum." This is absence-of-evidence vs evidence-of-absence error.*

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for catching this important interpretive error. We have revised the political-ideology moderator interpretation to: "The interaction with political ideology was not significant (β_interaction = 0.08, 95% CI [-0.02, 0.18], p = .12). The 95% CI includes meaningful interaction magnitudes, so we cannot rule out moderate ideological moderation; our study was powered (1-β = 0.80) only to detect interaction effects of β ≥ 0.16. Future studies with larger samples are needed to characterize moderation more precisely."

**Revision:** §3 Results (interaction-analysis subsection); §4 Discussion (paragraph 5).

#### R1.4 — Policy implications

> *The discussion's policy recommendations [...] assume generalizability the data don't support. The college-aged sample doesn't represent the populations whose trust dynamics policy targets.*

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for this important critique. We have substantially revised the discussion's policy implications. The strong claim ("policymakers should encourage community-based news consumption") has been moved to a new subsection labeled "Speculative implications for non-sampled populations" with explicit acknowledgment that the inference is generalization rather than direct evidence. The main policy implications now state only what the sample supports: that within college-aged populations, community participation correlates with trust, and that interventions might be most credibly targeted to similar populations.

**Revision:** §4 Discussion (paragraphs 6-7, restructured); new §4.3 "Speculative implications" subsection.

### Reviewer 2

#### R2.1 — Literature engagement + disagreement on causal language

> *I disagree with R1.1's framing — I think the causal language is fine if the authors clearly note the design's limitations. The bigger issue is that the literature review misses key prior work by Kraut & Resnick (2012) and others.*

**Response:** We appreciate Reviewer 2's careful reading and engagement with the framing question. We respectfully disagree on the causal-language point: the field has been moving toward more conservative associational language for cross-sectional designs (see Imai et al. 2024, *Methods in Hypothetical Research*), and we believe the revised framing strengthens the paper's credibility with readers across the discipline. We hope Reviewer 2 will find the revision retains the substantive contribution even with the more conservative phrasing.

On the literature point, we agree fully — we have added substantive engagement with Kraut & Resnick (2012) in §2 (Literature Review), positioned our work as both extending and complicating their account, and added three additional prior empirical studies (Lin et al. 2019; Nguyen & Park 2022; Hwang et al. 2024) that bear directly on the question.

**Revision:** §2 Literature Review — substantially expanded (new paragraphs 2-3).

#### R2.2 — Attention-check sensitivity

> *Please report attention-check pass rates and provide sensitivity analysis excluding inattentive participants.*

**Response:** We thank the reviewer for this methodological suggestion. We had two attention checks embedded in the survey (Q14 and Q22). 391 of 412 participants (94.9%) passed both. We have added these pass rates to §2 (Methods) and added a sensitivity analysis to §3 (Results): excluding the 21 participants who failed at least one attention check, the primary association estimate changes from β = 0.34 (95% CI [0.22, 0.46]) to β = 0.32 (95% CI [0.20, 0.44]) — essentially unchanged.

**Revision:** §2 Methods (attention-check protocol now described); §3 Results (sensitivity-analysis subsection added).

#### R2.3 — Convergent validity

> *The trust measurement is single-source (one validated instrument). Convergent validity with a second instrument would strengthen the result.*

**Response:** We agree this would strengthen the paper. We don't have a second trust measurement in this dataset, so we cannot run convergent-validity analysis on the current data. We have added a Limitations paragraph acknowledging this and noting that future work should include multiple trust instruments. **Note that this is one comment we could not fully address within the scope of this revision; we have explicitly flagged it.**

**Revision:** §5 Limitations (paragraph 3); §6 Future Directions (mentions multi-instrument approach as concrete next step).
